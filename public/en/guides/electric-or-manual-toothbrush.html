Electric or manual toothbrush? Choosing the right brush and brushing technique

A comparison of electric and manual brushes, which suits whom, choosing a brush (bristle stiffness, head size), correct technique, timing, toothpaste amount and common mistakes.

Written by: Dt. Aycan Işık Taş · Published: 14 September 2026

In short

A manual brush used correctly for 2 minutes is sufficient; studies do, however, show that electric brushes (especially oscillating-rotating ones) reduce plaque and gingivitis slightly more on average. An electric brush is particularly helpful for people with limited dexterity, orthodontic patients, hard brushers and those who struggle with motivation. Soft bristles and a small head are recommended for both types.

What does the evidence say?

The summary of many studies comparing electric and manual brushes: electric brushes give a small but consistent advantage over manual ones in plaque and gingivitis at 3 months. The difference comes less from the brush's "power" than from the standardised motion and helpers such as timers and pressure alerts. A manual brush used with good technique is also sufficient to keep the gums healthy; what really matters is technique, time and regularity.

Who benefits most from an electric brush?

  • People with limited hand or shoulder movement, older people
  • Those wearing braces or implant-supported prostheses
  • Hard brushers with gum recession (models with a pressure sensor)
  • Those who struggle to reach 2 minutes (timer)
  • Children brushed with a parent's help

Choosing a brush

  • Bristle stiffness: Soft. Hard bristles do not clean plaque better; they wear enamel and gums.
  • Head size: Small; it must reach behind the last teeth.
  • Replacement: Every 3 months or when the bristles splay; after an illness.
  • Electric type: Oscillating-rotating (round head) and sonic types are both effective; ergonomics and price decide. Cheap "battery vibrating" models do not count as real electric brushes.

Correct technique (manual)

  1. Place the brush at 45° to the gum margin.
  2. Brush a 2–3-tooth section for 10–15 seconds with short, small vibrating strokes, then sweep from gum to tooth.
  3. Outer surfaces, inner surfaces, chewing surfaces — in turn for each jaw; 2 minutes in all.
  4. Hold the brush vertically for the inside of the front teeth.
  5. Brush the tongue or use a tongue cleaner.

With an electric brush: Don't scrub; hold it on each tooth for 2–3 seconds and move on. If the pressure light comes on, ease off.

Common mistakes

  • A horizontal, hard "sawing" motion — gum recession and cervical notches
  • Finishing in 30–45 seconds (which the average person does)
  • Brushing straight after acidic drinks and fruit — wait 30 minutes
  • Rinsing with lots of water after brushing — reduces the effect of the fluoride in the paste; spitting without rinsing is better
  • Skipping the spaces between the teeth — the brush cannot get in; floss or an interdental brush is essential. See How to floss

Toothpaste and fluoride

For adults, a pea-sized amount of paste containing 1,350–1,500 ppm fluoride. For children, a rice-grain smear under 3 and pea size from 3 to 6, under parental supervision. Whitening pastes can be abrasive; ask your dentist if you have sensitivity. More: How to teach a child to brush

When to brush

In the morning after (or before) breakfast and at night before bed; the night-time brush matters most because saliva falls during sleep. Brushing after lunch adds benefit but is not essential; sugar-free gum helps.
